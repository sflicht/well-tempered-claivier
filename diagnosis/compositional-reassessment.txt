THE WELL-TEMPERED clAIvier — COMPOSITIONAL REASSESSMENT

Basis: the final second-edition event scores. This is direct score analysis,
not an auditory review. Quarter-beat positions are zero-based. The companion
xxiv-actual-texture.json records the exact source hash and calculations.

DECISION

XXIV does not fulfill the artistic brief of a fugue in 25 independent parts.
It is more accurately a fugal study distributed across 25 staves, culminating
in a largely chordal texture around a single thematic line. Its complete
subject entries establish that the staves have participated in an imitation;
they do not establish sustained contrapuntal identity.

The user's distinction between rhythmic interest and rhythmic zest is also
well supported by the writing. Different durations have been added without
rebuilding the gesture, metrical direction, phrase continuation, and harmonic
pressure that would make those durations consequential.

XXIV: WHAT THE NOTES ACTUALLY DO

The first complete 25-part span runs from quarter beat 412 to 484, or bars
104–121. All 25 staves sound throughout. Yet the sounding aggregate contains
exactly three pitch classes at every moment. There are 800 note attacks,
all on integer quarter beats. Among 775 consecutive within-part transitions,
only seven move by a semitone or whole tone; 103 move by an octave. The mean
number of distinct sounding pitches is 12.60, with the 25 staves sharing and
redistributing these pitches.

In fact, from beat 412 through 516 every sounding note belongs to the
currently declared three-note chord. There are no non-chord passing tones
or prepared suspensions in this entire 104-beat climactic region.

These statistics are not a general definition of counterpoint. A sustained
tonic or a triadic passage can be expressive, and quarter-note rhythms can
have great force. Here the statistics identify a specific compositional
mechanism visible in the individual parts: almost continuous redistribution
of current chord tones, rather than connected melodic arguments.

Examples at the start of the tutti:

- Voice 1 has the augmented subject from beat 412 to 436. No other voice
  has a complete subject or countersubject anywhere in that 24-beat span.
- Voice 2 plays B5 for three beats, D5 for one, B5 for six, B4 for two,
  B5 for five. These jumps place chord members in different registers;
  they have little relation to a developed melodic sentence.
- Voice 25 plays B1 for six beats, G1 for six, then B1 for twenty beats.
  It supplies a bass foundation, not a continuing fugal line.
- The harmony is uninterrupted tonic from beat 426 to 452: 26 quarter
  beats across the labels 'Complete ensemble' and 'Gathered development'.
  Changing section labels and adding attacks do not give this prolongation
  a new rhetorical direction.
- From beat 444 to 516, the only exact thematic annotations are two
  two-note head fragments, at beats 444 and 500. There is no complete
  subject or countersubject in that 72-beat paragraph.
- The long dominant, beats 484–500, has 20 sounding parts, exactly three
  pitch classes at each instant, no attack between quarter beats, and no
  complete theme or annotated fragment. It is held and redistributed
  dominant harmony; its length is not itself an intensification.

The present design contains five successive expositions for five groups,
followed by a small number of further entries and a massed harmonic arrival.
The second edition retained more earlier voices, but most retained voices
were generated as permissible chord-tone continuations. Their pitch changes
made the occupancy statistics look better while leaving the basic problem
intact. There are no duplicated whole-part files, and the rhythmic onset
patterns in the first tutti differ; neither fact establishes musical
independence.

The constraint system's mistake was conceptual. It could forbid certain
bad motions and reward motion among available chord tones. It could not
decide why a line should make this gesture, at this moment, toward this
destination. Passing the screen was then allowed to stand in for that
missing compositional judgment.

PRELUDE IV: SECTIONAL ACTIVITY WITHOUT A COMPELLING CELL

The opening upper line is C-sharp5 dotted half, G-sharp5 quarter; then
A5–G-sharp5–F-sharp5–E5 in quarters; then D-sharp5 dotted quarter,
E5 eighth, F-sharp5 quarter, D-sharp5 quarter; then E5 dotted half and a rest.
Below it, long bass notes and inner chord members largely confirm each
bar's harmony. This establishes a broad tune over accompaniment, but the
rhythm has little initial impulse that demands the next phrase.

Bar 9 changes the inner part into a regular E-major arpeggio. Bar 17
changes the upper part into running sixteenths over a held tonic bass.
Those are changes of surface activity, not clear consequences of the
opening gesture. The later return is raised into a brighter register, but
the extra height substitutes too readily for thematic transformation.

The 'Invocation', 'First light', and 'Current' labels propose an expressive
narrative more strongly than the musical transitions establish one. A new
prelude should earn those changes through the development of a specific
cell, a stronger bass trajectory, and phrase endings that create the need
for the following phrase.

FUGUE I: A SUBJECT THAT ENDS BEFORE IT HAS GENERATED MUCH

Its subject is C5–E5–G5 in quarters, D5–E5 in eighths, F5 quarter,
E5–D5 in eighths, and C5 half. It makes a complete tonic excursion and
settles back onto the tonic. This is usable material, but its rounded shape
and emphatic closure give the next voice little urgent unfinished business.

The surrounding episodes can be labeled as fragments and the answer can be
verified note-for-note while the piece still lacks a strong rhythmic motor.
The replacement should make the bass an interlocutor: an entry, interruption,
continuation, or harmonic challenge should change what the upper line does.
A recurring eighth/sixteenth figure needs an accentual shape and a destination;
more short notes are not a substitute for those things.

A DIFFERENT ARCHITECTURE FOR THE EVENTUAL 25-PART WORK

Begin by composing the actual contrapuntal combination that will justify
the climax. Work backwards from that combination to the expositions and
forward through the release. Do not write 25 separate introductory entries
and hope that the last tutti will retroactively make them necessary.

The material needs several compatible but strongly contrasting behaviours:
for example, a broad subject, an urgent upbeat figure, a chain of prepared
suspensions, a repeated-note rhythm, and a mobile bass line. These are
possible compositional roles, not a formula of five choirs times five parts.
The roles must migrate between parts; each part must keep its own phrase
and registral continuity while the listener's attention moves among them.

First make a four- or five-part combination that is musically persuasive
without orchestration. Establish the particular inversions, entry distances,
and exchanges that its actual notes permit. Extend that material to eight
and twelve parts, then further, only where the additions create a new
relationship. Doubling a chord member, relocating a line, or splitting one
musical role between more staves is not an increase in contrapuntal substance.

The approach to the climax should let several unfinished phrases accumulate
pressure together. Useful means include closer but intelligible entries,
prepared dissonances whose resolutions generate further suspensions,
changes of harmonic pace, and an arrival postponed for a specific melodic
reason. A common final cadence can then resolve several lines at once.
Beginning a loud tutti on an already-settled tonic cannot supply that history.

Rhythmic zest must be authored at the level of gesture: an upbeat that
leans into a destination, an accent that another voice answers, an apparently
finished phrase carried onward by an overlapping entry, and a cadence whose
release is felt against continuing motion. Metric clarity and variation
must cooperate. Random displacement and constant sixteenths would repeat the
same methodological failure in a faster surface.

The next useful review is therefore a close examination of the new small
pieces: rhythmic identity, harmonic necessity, phrase connection, genuine
exchange between parts, and any literal bad notes. Numerical summaries may
expose defects, as they did here; they cannot certify that those defects have
been replaced by convincing music.

FOLLOW-UP: WHAT TO RETAIN IN III AND VIII

Sam's positive listening response to Preludes III and VIII matters. The score
offers specific compositional identities worth preserving; the response is
not a reason to declare the whole existing method successful, or to rewrite
every prelude into the same faster texture.

Prelude III, in C-sharp major and 12/8, maintains a sparse bell-like mechanism.
Within its opening six-quarter-beat bar the upper attacks occur at 0, 1.5,
3.5 and 5; the upper inner part answers at 0.5, 2.5 and 4.5, and the lower
inner part at 1 and 4. The bass holds across the six beats. The notes leave
space, so the shifting attacks and registral relations constitute the piece's
identity. That mechanism survives changes of harmony and register and the
final gathering of the separate strands into a sustained chord. It is a
coherent textural idea; its promise does not depend on a claim of strict
four-part fugue counterpoint. Preserve the space and the recognisable figure.

Prelude VIII, in E-flat minor and 12/8, contrasts continuous inner eighth
notes with a slower upper melody. Its opening B-flat–A-flat–G-flat is a
falling gesture rather than an isolated chord-member selection. The following
bar embellishes G-flat and reaches A-flat before returning; the slow line can
continue while the inner motion sustains the compound pulse. At bar 11 the
upper A-flat is genuinely held across the new harmony before resolving to
G-flat. Preserve this separation of time scales and the falling-and-returning
melodic arc. Its expressive potential does not require more busy notes.

Fugue VIII's attractive climax and its failure of nine-part counterpoint
are compatible judgments. At q184–216, bars 47–54, the first full ensemble
contains no complete subject or countersubject annotation. Its lowest
E-flat2 is held from q184 to 200, for sixteen quarter beats. Several inner
lines cycle through E-flat/G-flat/B-flat and then members of the next chord.
For instance, Voice 3 repeatedly alternates G-flat4 and E-flat4; Voice 5
holds G-flat3 for nine beats beginning at q191. The upper registral opening
and the return of the full sonority after a bar of silence can provide an
effective arrival, which the user indeed likes.

Unlike XXIV's first tutti, this passage does include fractional-beat passing
motion: sixteen attacks fall between quarter beats, and passing tones at
times enlarge the three-pitch-class aggregate. These details improve the
surface but do not turn nine participating staves into nine continuing
thematic arguments. The useful revision should retain the arrival's pacing
and sonority while composing actual interacting phrases into its supporting
parts. It should not destroy a successful harmonic moment merely because
that moment was mislabeled as contrapuntal achievement.

FOLLOW-UP: WHY THE FUGUES SOUND ALIKE

The further listening criticism that the fugues sound alike is supported by
their shared compositional machinery. The differences in their named subjects,
keys and tempos are real, but these parameters sit inside nearly the same
phrase design, harmonic vocabulary, formal sequence and method of filling
supporting parts. This is a failure of the model of composition, not a need
for another cosmetic rhythmic pass.

The source compose_cycle_fugues.py contains 23 generated subject designs;
Fugue IV was handled separately. All 23 begin on the tonic at beat zero and
end on a tonic half note. Twenty are exactly twelve quarter beats long; the
other three are eight. Their original durations are exclusively quarters and
halves. Their differing melodic contours therefore share the same emphatic
start, limited rhythmic vocabulary and rounded terminal gesture. The two-beat
tonic ending was explicitly selected to make the same terminal-tail overlap
available in every fugue. The requirement of an easily solved combination
has shaped the subjects toward one generic rhetorical profile.

The common formal plan in FugueBuilder.compose is equally consequential:
group expositions of at most five voices; a four-chord episode; related-key
entries; the two-beat tail overlap; a bar of silence; a common-tone tutti;
a final return and cadence. Entry order, episode length and key order change
according to voice count or the movement number modulo three, rather than
according to what the particular subject demands. The major episodes use
vi–ii–V–I; the minor episodes VI–iv–V–i. The same-mode related-key entries
generally move to keys five and seven semitones above the home tonic. Every
score generated here is in 4/4. The bright-major/slower-minor tempo pattern
adds a broad binary distinction, not twenty-three different personalities.

The original recurring countersubject is also solved, not independently
invented: its simultaneous intervals with the subject are restricted to
thirds and sixths, and its pitches are selected using the same register,
step-distance and generic contour preferences. Ordinary episodes pin no
subject-derived melodic fragment; the solver is handed a chord progression
and asked for legal motion. The title 'Sequential episode' does not make
those motions a necessary development of this fugue's material.

The dense-section code makes the fundamental error especially explicit.
dense_episode first writes a three-part harmonic core, then inserts the
other staves one at a time from the current chord's pitch classes, requiring
a minimum number of changes. Its standard harmony is I–vi–IV–I, or i–VI–iv–i,
with eight beats per chord. XXIV's special variant pins a single augmented
subject over a four-part core and fills the other twenty-one staves around
it. Later revisions retain these cores and replace or extend the filler.
Calling a part 'independent' because it changes pitch several times mistakes
activity for a connected musical thought.

The rhythmic revision reinforces why the second edition could not repair
this. revise_rhythmic_counterpoint.py preserves the existing anchors and
applies a quota of one subject alteration to even-numbered pieces, two to
odd-numbered pieces, and up to three to the countersubject. Candidate changes
fill an existing leap, ornament a held note or delay an existing resolution.
The choices are screened for not adding an interval finding. That can improve
an individual connection, but it cannot supply a missing phrase argument or
make twenty-three forms grow out of twenty-three different subjects.

The new edition must therefore not be assembled from this expansion method.
Increasing the number of staves must increase the number of meaningful
simultaneous relationships, not merely the number of chord-tone constraints.
Each new fugue needs its own distinctive subject, answer, continuation and
formal necessity. The final dense combination must be composed as interacting
phrases before it can justify a twenty-five-part exposition or climax. A good
two-voice study can begin rebuilding compositional judgment, but it does not
prove that the old system will scale successfully after all.
