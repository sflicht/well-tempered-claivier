THE WELL-TEMPERED clAIvier
Listener-led reassessment and a reset of the fugue method

STATUS

The existing cycle does not meet the requested artistic or contrapuntal
standard. The claim that its twenty-five-staff finale establishes real
twenty-five-part counterpoint is withdrawn. The same problem affects the
nine-part climax of Fugue VIII. Its harmonic arrival can be valuable while
the surrounding lines still fail the contrapuntal brief.

The preludes are the more promising strand according to the listener. III
and VIII should be retained as positive reference pieces. The task is now
selective development of the preludes and a fundamental restart of the
fugue method. This package contains two short new audition studies and the
reasoning behind that restart. It is not a third complete edition.

1. WHY THE FUGUES SOUND TOO SIMILAR

The sameness is built into the source. All twenty-four existing fugues use
4/4. Twenty-three subjects produced by the common generator end with the
same two-beat tonic. Many begin with a tonic-to-third or tonic-to-fifth
gesture, using a closely related quarter/half-note vocabulary. Their
minor and major variants use different pitches and tempos, but the common
construction gives them very similar phrase behaviour.

The shared builder supplies successive entries, similar related-key
statements, similar episodes, a minimal overlap over the terminal tonic,
a full-ensemble paragraph, a return and a cadence. It makes these choices
before the particular subject has earned them. The later rhythmic pass
preserves the same anchors and inserts admissible passing figures or
delayed notes. That cannot create a new large-scale argument.

Common metre is not itself a defect. The deeper issue is that the subjects
are repeatedly made to perform the same dramatic job, and the form rarely
changes in response to their particular musical possibilities.

Source evidence: tools/compose_cycle_fugues.py, especially THEMES,
FugueBuilder.compose(), episode(), stretto(), and ensemble(); and
tools/revise_rhythmic_counterpoint.py. The relevant old source is included
as historical evidence in the package. It is not the new composition method.

2. WHY THE MANY-PART CONSTRUCTION FAILS

The old solver selects a triad at each quarter-note position, restricts
free pitches to that chord, then rewards inexpensive motion around a
shared contour. For dense music it writes a three- or four-part core and
inserts the remaining lines against that fixed harmony. A minimum number
of pitch changes becomes a proxy for a continuing musical part.

This gives the added lines an inferior musical status from the outset:
they cannot substantially change the phrases, harmonic implications or
destination of the already completed core. They can be technically
different tracks while doing little beyond redistributing chord tones.

In XXIV's first sustained full-ensemble span, bars 104-121, every sounding
instant contains only three pitch classes. There is almost no stepwise
movement, and the bass includes a twenty-beat B. The high subject is
accompanied by many chord-member movements rather than a substantial
network of other developing phrases. In VIII's first full ensemble,
bars 47-54, the E-flat bass lasts four bars and several inner voices
recycle the triad. VIII has some passing motion and the listener likes
the arrival; this remains a reason to preserve that arrival.

A pedal, triad or long note is not intrinsically bad counterpoint. Nor
must every part continually state a complete subject. The problem here
is what the actual surrounding lines do over time. Numerical variety,
absence of identical tracks and successful interval screens do not supply
the missing musical relationships.

3. WHAT A DIFFERENT METHOD MUST COMPOSE

Begin with a particular difficult contrapuntal encounter, not a quota of
tracks. For Fugue VIII, the first proof should be a convincing nine-line
paragraph that travels into and through the valued arrival. Write the
actual thematic material and continuations needed for that paragraph.
Then derive the exposition, episodes and approach from the relationships
the listener needs to learn before that encounter can make its effect.

At every stage all lines remain revisable. A new bass can turn a held
upper consonance into a suspension and require a different resolution;
an entering subject can redirect an unfinished phrase; a returning
fragment can become the continuation that carries the harmony onward.
The new voice participates in making those decisions. It does not fill
an empty lane around decisions that are already complete.

For twenty-five parts, a multiple-subject design with contrasting rates
of motion is a plausible research direction. Broad thematic lines,
quicker imitative material, suspensions and shorter responses can coexist.
These functions should move among the parts. Five isolated five-part
choirs, added together, would leave the central question unanswered:
what new contrapuntal relations occur when the ensembles meet?

The full combination needs its own harmonic journey. A static tonic
field that accommodates twenty-five lines is not sufficient. A smaller
combination is useful for learning the material's possibilities, but it
must remain open to rewriting as the complete texture develops.

The target is sustained musical agency in all the parts, with clear
foreground and background that can change. Equal busyness in every
voice would be another poor design. Rests, broad notes and selective
attention are legitimate; permanent harmonic padding counted as extra
counterpoint is the failure to avoid.

4. WHERE COMPUTATION CAN HELP

The search object should be a placement or transformation of a composed
phrase. Software can explore entry intervals and distances, inversions,
registral exchanges and deliberately chosen augmentations. It can expose
exactly where a proposed combination produces a bad melodic join, loses
a suspension's preparation, or frustrates a directed note's resolution.

There are 300 unordered voice pairs in twenty-five parts. Checking them
is useful, but pairwise compatibility alone is insufficient: the current
bass, metre, preparation and continuation determine how intervals function
in the entire texture. A new lower voice can change the interpretation
of two upper lines that previously worked together.

The program must be able to report that a musical proposal does not work.
It must not quietly rescue every proposal by changing difficult notes
into chord tones, holding a common tone, or thinning the music to three
significant lines. Composer and critic must then revise or reject the
actual musical idea. No computed score can decide whether its arrival
feels earned.

The companion document fugue-method-from-scratch.txt gives a concrete
three-line suspension example and detailed failure conditions.

5. RHYTHMIC ZEST AND EXPRESSIVE ACCUMULATION

The earlier revision confused more rhythmic events with more momentum.
An even stream can be alive when its accents, contour and harmonic
destination give it direction. Irregular durations can remain inert.
The phrase needs an impulse, an obstacle or delay, and a continuation.
Another voice should be able to interrupt, answer, complete or redirect it.

Bach's five-part C-sharp-minor fugue, BWV 849, provides a useful score
reference. Its broad thematic motion and flowing eighth-note material
have distinct identities, and their functions migrate through the parts.
The separate-staff score makes the alternation of activity, holding and
rest particularly clear. The compositional lesson drawn here is to plan
specific encounters between recognizable behaviours, rather than to
equate the number of busy staves with intensity.

Primary score consulted: J. S. Bach, BWV 849, separate-staff edition,
https://www.musanim.com/pdf/bwv849f_openScore.pdf
Also: Mutopia edition, source Breitkopf & Hartel, 1866,
https://www.mutopiaproject.org/ftp/BachJS/BWV849/bwv849b/bwv849b-a4.pdf

The desired ecstatic progression should be earned by accumulating
unfinished phrases, closer meaningful entries, prepared dissonance,
changing harmonic pace and an arrival that resolves several histories
together. Increasing volume or register can support that event once the
lines have created it. It cannot supply the event on its own.

6. WHAT THE TWO NEW AUDITIONS ACTUALLY CHANGE

Fugue I in C major: 25 bars of 4/4, quarter = 116; 51.7 seconds of
written music. Its subject begins on an eighth-note upbeat and ends on
B, continuing into C as the next phrase begins. A recurring longer-note
companion is exchanged above and below the subject. The bass initiates
a developmental sequence and leads the tonic return. A descending-third
development passes the thematic head into the lower voice. The closing
tail returns to the subject's rising motion.

Prelude IV in C-sharp minor: 32 bars of 3/4, quarter = 96; 60 seconds of
written music. One springing gesture drives toward a note held across
the third beat, followed by a descending tail. The bass takes the gesture
in bars 5-6 and 17-18. A rising bridge restores the register before the
return. Prepared dissonances and actual phrase joins were reviewed, with
a final E anticipation completing the approach to the E-major statement.

Both studies use literal phrase-level note authorship. That is a change
in construction, not evidence that the music is good. They remain compact,
conventional tonal studies with artistic risks. The new Fugue I is not a
prototype automatically expandable into larger fugues, and neither piece
has been accepted as a replacement in a completed third edition.

7. CURRENT LIMITS AND THE NEW NINE-PLAYER EXPERIMENT

The separate 20-bar experiment "The procession finds a dance floor" now
offers a more adventurous many-part audition: a measured Dies irae head,
an original clipped dance, and a broad rising idea exchange instrumental
roles. Its D-centered modal language bends through E-flat before a major
release. The traditional eight-note chant fragment is credited in the
score; the dance and rising idea are original.

The first checkpoint repeated a central flaw of the old approach: several
parallel bridge statements and an octave-doubled chant made the first
nine-player entrance behave as grouped harmony. The revision replaces
those alignments with delayed imitation, a twelve-beat cello phrase,
distinct violin and bass continuations, and different trajectories at
the return. A short shared octave gesture is retained as an arrival color;
it is not counted as proof of an additional independent melodic path.

The clearest interactions are specific. E-natural falls to E-flat and D
successively in viola, horn and bassoon. At beat 48 the flute hurries the
chant; the violin follows a beat later. The dance head starts in horn at
48, double bass at 49 and cello at 50, then those low parts continue in
different directions. This is more than a count of moving staves, but
the passage remains an audition experiment, not a complete Fugue VIII
or a demonstration of successful twenty-five-part writing.

No revised twenty-five-part fugue has been composed in this reset. The
many-part problem still includes developing convincing material into a
complete Fugue VIII and, eventually, the larger works. Preserve the liked
arrival as a destination to earn, without treating its old inner writing
as compulsory.

Before another full cycle is assembled, independently conceived fugues
must establish distinct characters and forms through their actual notes.
New titles, different metres and instruments are insufficient evidence.
The material should make different forms necessary.

Direct audio input was unavailable in this assistant session. The work
was reviewed through the score; recordings were generated and technically
decoded. Those actions are not a listening assessment. The listener's
reports remain essential evidence of the result's perceptual success.

All three new auditions have exact-note animated performances with brief
listening captions. Their note displays and thematic cues follow the
written score. The complete revised cycle and monumental-space film remain
project goals. Existing visual assets are not evidence that the underlying
many-part music succeeds.

8. THE BEETHOVEN REFERENCE: GIVE FUGAL WRITING A DRAMATIC PURPOSE

Sam supplied https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=i7lr1cSQi5o and clarified that
the intended inspiration is Beethoven's creative use of fugal passages in
his symphonies. The video was not directly watched or heard in this session.
The following is an application of Sam's point to this project, rather than
a report of that video's specific examples.

Each fugue needs a particular reason to unfold as it does. Possible
contrasting premises include a dance whose imitation gradually overturns
its accents; a lament whose long phrases gather into a suspension chain;
two previously separate ideas whose eventual combination supplies the
arrival; or a subject that survives interruption and returns transformed.
These are candidate musical problems, not a new set of titles to paste
onto the same generator. The actual notes must establish the premise.

This changes how form should be chosen. A subject need not always receive
the same number and spacing of answers, followed by the same episode and
climax. Material can demand a compressed exposition, an extended episode,
a delayed combination, a sudden thinning, or a return that changes the
meaning of the opening. The varied formal functions need to arise from
the particular subjects and their interactions.

The twenty-four works should therefore be conceived individually. Their
shared key cycle and increasing number of voices give them an overarching
design; they do not require a shared sequence of compositional events.
The high-part works still need the promised sustained real counterpoint.
A dramatic orchestral tutti or a brief fugal allusion would not satisfy
that constraint by itself.

For VIII, this suggests making the valued harmonic arrival the consequence
of earlier contrapuntal activity, with a distinctive route into it. For
XXIV, the eventual convergence should transform material the listener has
learned, creating new relationships when the ensemble meets. This is the
compositional use of the monumental space and orchestration to pursue once
the musical relations themselves have been written and heard.

9. CREATIVE FREEDOM

Sam subsequently made the latitude explicit: this project can define an
AI Fugue genre, with original tunes, borrowed melodies, recycled AI material,
cheeky allusions and nonclassical influences available as choices. None is
mandatory. The aim is forty-eight pieces with character, pleasure, surprise
and the possibility of grandeur. The accompanying ARTISTIC_DIRECTION.txt
sets out a permissive direction rather than another specification to pass.
The critical findings above retire a failed method; they should not make
the new music timid or uniformly solemn.
