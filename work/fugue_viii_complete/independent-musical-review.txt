SCOPE AND VERDICT
This is a review of the literal score and its combinations, not an audio
listening evaluation. I examined pitches, durations, overlapping phrases,
imitative entries, selected low-register dissonances, and harmonic arrivals.
All beat numbers below count quarter notes from zero; pitches are concert
pitches in the saved E-flat score.

The 400-beat piece now contains substantial nine-part imitative writing and
an intelligible route from exposed chant through dance, disturbance, retreat,
return, and a delayed major-mode arch. Its claim to counterpoint has concrete
support in the notes. The nine tracks and their entry labels alone would not
establish that claim. This remains an audition, with no certification of
Bach-level invention, beauty, or the listener's emotional response.

WHAT MAKES THE DENSE WRITING MORE CREDIBLE
The chant begins alone in the horn. Its eight-beat opening is heard before
the first answer, and the dance interrupts later rather than competing from
the first note. Every instrument has carried a complete chant statement by
beat 100.

The first dense paragraph around 80-104 combines recognizable material with
continuations that have different lengths and destinations. The revision to
the flute at 88-90 creates an actual held-and-resolved gesture; the cello at
92-104 reaches and holds its apex before its neighbors finish. These changes
address a real weakness of the earlier quarter-note marching, rather than
merely adding more attacks.

At 208-224, flute and violin begin compressed chant statements one beat
apart. Horn, double bass and cello exchange the dance head at 208, 209 and

example of shared material becoming several concurrent musical actions.

The return from 288 uses overlapping 8-, 12- and 16-beat sentences and delayed

crosses longer lines whose peaks and releases occur at different times.
The flute reaches its highest held point at 360-363; several other parts
are still rising or completing sentences. The ensemble settles into its

DISSONANCE AND HARMONIC DIRECTION
The staggered chromatic F-Fb-Eb motions beginning at 176 are intentionally
separated across instruments and octaves. Their successive semitone motions
provide a reason for the friction; they should not be flattened into simple
chords merely because their notes overlap.

The relative-major arrival is explicit: Db7 at 111 leads to Gb at 112.
The dense dark section reaches Bb7 at 239; its clarinet seventh resolves to
Gb at 240 as the ensemble thins and the cello begins the enlarged memory.
The final bass route is Ab at 384, F at 388, Bb at 392 and Eb at 396, with
the borrowed Cb helping darken the F half-diminished region before release.
The fourth, ninth and leading-note gestures resolve at different times over
the final tonic. Extended vertical sonorities are part of this idiom;
the low-register repairs above distinguish directed friction from accidents.

LIMITS TO KEEP IN VIEW DURING LISTENING
Several continuations still favor stepwise scalar arches and quarter-note
motion. Differing schedules do not by themselves make nine equally memorable
melodies. The deliberate octave presentations at 48-56 and 144-152 are colors,
not evidence of independence. The rendering must make the important handoffs
audible, especially inside 208-224 and 352-376; source inspection cannot tell
us whether the mix achieves that or whether the late arrival moves a listener.

No further local score repair is recommended before a complete audition.
The useful next evidence is what can actually be followed and felt in the
rendered performance, rather than another numerical voice-count certificate.
