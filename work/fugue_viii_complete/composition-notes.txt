THE PIECE

This is a complete nine-part Fugue VIII in E-flat minor, arriving in E-flat
major. It lasts 400 quarter-note beats at quarter = 108: 3 minutes 42.22
seconds of written music, before the rendered release. Its nine monophonic
parts are flute, oboe, clarinet, violin, viola, horn, bassoon, cello and double
bass. The open score is at sounding pitch. A piano rendering can use the same
notes; the chamber colors make the exchanges easier to distinguish.

The piece grows from the earlier twenty-bar experiment into a new hundred-bar
form. Its argument is a solemn procession interrupted by a dance, unsettled by
a chromatic turn, suddenly hurried, then remembered almost alone. The return
combines those experiences. The final rising figure takes much longer to reach
its high point than the short dance heads moving beneath it.

The composition is written as literal, revisable phrases. The later players
were not added as chord-compatible filling around an unchanged small ensemble.
Every player takes thematic material and also follows distinct continuations.
Some short passages deliberately share a melody in octaves. Those are changes
of color within the form; the sustained contrapuntal paragraphs depend on the
different entries, rhythms, contours, continuations and endings around them.

THE THREE RECURRING IDEAS

1. The measured chant. The opening eight-note Dies irae head appears here as
   G-flat, F, G-flat, E-flat, F, D-flat, E-flat, E-flat. Its measured rhythm,
   modal changes, register, continuations and transformations are newly
   composed. The historical melody is credited in the score data, with the
   D-centered reference pitches distinguished from this edition's concert
   pitches. This is a traditional melody quotation, not a Bach quotation.

2. The clipped dance. Its head is E-flat, F, G-flat, B-flat, A-flat, G-flat,
   E-flat, B-flat. Accents fall at the beginning, after one and a half beats,
   and after three beats: a 3 + 3 + 2 grouping of half-beats. One short-long
   displacement inside the head prevents it becoming an evenly tapped scale.
   The dance can enter away from a bar boundary; its accents follow its own
   entrance. Its complete eight-beat statement and its four-beat head are
   labeled separately.

3. The broad rising figure. B-flat rises through E-flat, F and G-flat toward
   another B-flat, then falls. It begins as a companion to the chant and later
   resists the fast chase. In the final major section it acquires G-natural
   and D-natural, and the flute doubles its note lengths. That expansion
   supplies the delayed crest rather than merely adding more simultaneous
   notes to the dance.

REVISION RECORD

Two independent score reviews examined formal pacing and local counterpoint.
They led to specific changes rather than a blanket removal of dissonance:

- The early cello route was rewritten where it had doubled the bassoon and
  then split into a low semitone without a useful resolution.
- The flute acquired a held-note resolution across the moving bass; the
  cello's ascent acquired an early, longer apex. These changes reduced an
  overly regular quarter-note march within the first dense paragraph.
- The bassoon's gathering tail was rewritten as a sustained and turning
  line where it had converged into the viola's same unison descent.
- Four later low-register points were clarified: one bassoon pitch, two
  cello pitches, and a shorter cello appoggiatura followed by its resolution.
- The deliberately displaced chromatic cascade was retained. Its friction
  belongs to moving imitated lines and is part of the dramatic disturbance.
- Final-bar descriptions were corrected to call the new attacks
  appoggiaturas or delayed resolutions, rather than prepared suspensions.
