FUGUE VIII — INDEPENDENT SCORE REVIEW OF THE FIRST NINE-PART PARAGRAPH
23 October 2026

SCOPE AND METHOD

This review compares the frozen complete score with the proposed passage
revision, concentrating on score beats 80–112 and its connection through
beat 114. The surrounding music at 64–80 and 114–128 supplies context.

I inspected actual note pitches, durations, simultaneous combinations,
registers, phrase continuations, and thematic annotations. I did not listen
to either rendition and did not use an external audio model. This report
does not certify audible clarity, beauty, strict historical counterpoint,
or equivalence to Bach.

VERDICT

The revision gives this paragraph more specific melodic and rhythmic
activity, better distinguishes several previously merged lines, and turns
its arrival into overlapping resolutions. It is ready for a direct
before-and-after audition. I request no additional local note changes
before that comparison.

The evidence supports a meaningful improvement in the written construction.
It does not establish that all nine parts will be independently audible or
that the new passage sounds better.

WHAT THE ORIGINAL PARAGRAPH NEEDED

1. Many continuing lines followed a shared quarter-note gait. Viola and
   cello at beats 80–84 duplicated the same rising scale in octaves.
   Different tracks therefore did not always produce different musical
   behavior.

2. The cello dance head at 88–92 occupied Eb2–Bb2 among active lower parts.
   This created a plausible masking risk. That risk was inferred from the
   register and texture, not established by listening.

3. Several important moments merged voices: at 94 flute and oboe shared F5
   while lower parts reinforced F in octaves; at 100 oboe, clarinet, and
   violin attacked Ab4 together.

4. Most parts stopped together at 112. The Db7 at 111 was already functional
   and resolved to Gb at 112; it was not a harmonic mistake. The weakness
   was the broad simultaneous release of a thick texture.

WHAT THE REVISION CHANGES

A three-beat retort compresses and extends the chant's head. It appears in
clarinet at 80, violin at 82.5, cello at 85, viola at 88, and horn at 91.
Its dotted and shorter notes recur recognizably, while the starts cross
different positions in the four-beat measure. The following continuations
diverge rather than simply repeat one rhythmic scaffold.

This retort is properly described as a derived motif or fragment, not as
five new complete subject statements. The revised annotations reflect that
distinction.

The full bass chant at 80 and bassoon chant at 92 remain. Consequently the
shorter exchanges coexist with longer thematic sentences rather than
replacing all material with quick fragments.

The cello retort is raised into a tenor register. Its subsequent dance
head at 88–92 is one octave above the former low version. That should give
the two gestures a better opportunity to emerge, but the actual balance
still needs to be heard.

The revised cello continuation at 92–96 rises through Gb3, Bb3, Db4, and Eb4
before falling to Bb3. Oboe moves downward over the same span. The cello
reaches its apex later and sustains across a different boundary. This is a
concrete horizontal difference, not simply a different instrument patch.

The oboe's relative-major chant begins at 100 rather than 96. The flute
makes its higher entrance at 96, so these two events are no longer imposed
at the same point. The flute's short Ab6 at 109 in the first revision draft
was removed to leave more registral space for the work's later crest.

At the Gb arrival, the parts do not all stop at the bar line:
- Violin carries Cb5 from 111 across 112, resolving to Bb4 at 112.5.
- Horn carries Ab3 across 112, resolving to Gb3 at 112.5.
- Flute holds F6 from 110 through 112, rising to Gb6 at 113.

These notes belong to the preceding dominant and acquire different
relationships to the new bass. Their
delayed resolutions have an identifiable harmonic purpose. The flute's
upward resolution is a retardation; the violin and horn resolve downward.
They should not all be described indiscriminately as the same suspension.

REPAIRS MADE DURING THIS REVIEW

The first revised draft contained several local problems. The composer
implemented the following targeted changes, all confirmed in the final
reviewed file:

- The cello retort at 85–88 moved up an octave, avoiding its previous low
  Gb3 against sustained F3 in horn/bassoon.

- Bassoon at 86 now holds Gb3 for 1.5 beats before Eb3 at 87.5. This removes
  the Cb3/Bb2 semitone created when horn moved under the former bassoon note.

- Horn at 94 now plays Db3, and clarinet at 94.5 plays Db4. This avoids the
  old horn F3/bassoon Gb3 clash and the competing D/Db inflection during
  that short first-inversion sonority.

- Viola at 98 plays Db4 instead of Cb4, eliminating its full-beat minor
  second against horn Bb3 while preserving a distinct arcing continuation.

- Cello at 92–95.5 previously doubled oboe in exact octaves, including the
  same rest. Its new rising continuation removes that substantive copy.

- Flute at 109–110 now descends Eb6–Db6 before its F6 hold. The earlier Ab6
  would have brought this local arrival close to the later work's Bb6
  climax in register. This is a proportional form decision, not a rule
  that every early high note is prohibited.

These changes were chosen for local melodic or harmonic reasons. The
review did not attempt to eliminate all shared pitches, all dissonances,
or every simultaneous attack.

WHAT REMAINS UNCERTAIN OR IMPERFECT

The passage still uses expanded modal and tonal sonorities, occasional
parallel motion, transient unisons, and scalar movement. This was not an
exhaustive audit of every dissonance treatment or consecutive perfect
interval. There is no claim that its harmony follows all of Bach's
conventions. Its language should be described honestly as the project's
developing contrapuntal style.

The increased dotted-note and offbeat activity might create a more general
busy surface if the timbres and balance fail to expose the exchanges.
More differentiated notation does not guarantee stronger rhythmic zest
in the recording.

The high flute may still dominate while inner gestures recede. The cello's
new register is more favorable on paper, but an expressive handoff is an
auditory question. Nine parts need not remain nine equally prominent solo
lines at every instant; nevertheless, a listener should be able to follow
the intended changes of foreground.

The final simultaneous convergences and partial doubling near a cadence
are not automatically defects. The relevant question is whether the lines
have meaningful identities and destinations over the paragraph.

RECOMMENDED COMPARISON

Compare the same surrounding span, beats 64–128, in the original and
revised stereo chamber renditions with consistent playback treatment.
Listen for the three-beat retort passing between players, the cello's
independent ascent, and the resolutions continuing across the Gb arrival.
Use isolated parts only to learn those events, then return to the ensemble
to judge whether they communicate without visual assistance.

The complete form and final climax were not re-reviewed here. This is a
focused score review supporting an audition, not approval of the cycle.
