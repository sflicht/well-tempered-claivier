FUGUE VIII — A THREE-BEAT REPLY INSIDE NINE-PART WRITING
Passage revision, 23 October 2026

PURPOSE

This is a literal recomposition of the first sustained nine-part paragraph
of the 100-bar Fugue VIII, with a small extension across its following
cadence. It addresses the sameness of the free continuations, the hidden
cello reply, and the tendency for different written parts to behave alike.
It was not produced by randomizing rhythms, fitting extra lines to a fixed
small-voice core, or optimizing an independence score.

The source remains a working audition. Neither the composer nor the
independent score critic listened to the audio. Their judgments concern
written musical relations, not demonstrated perceptual clarity or beauty.
The previously attempted Fal listening reports did not direct this rewrite.

THE NEW MUSICAL DEVICE

A three-beat retort is derived from the chant's initial neighbor turn:
G-flat, F, G-flat, E-flat, B-flat, E-flat. Its durations are .75, .25, .5,
.5, .5, .5 quarter-note beats. The first four pitches preserve the chant's
turn; its compressed rhythm and dropped-fourth/return extension turn it
into a short question. The short-long relations are composed as a gesture.
They are not random accent offsets.

The retort enters at unequal distances:

Voice       Score beat   Full-recording time    Register
Clarinet       80           0:44.444             G-flat4 head
Violin         82.5         0:45.833             G-flat5 head
Cello          85           0:47.222             G-flat4 head
Viola          88           0:48.889             G-flat4 head
Horn           91           0:50.556             C-flat4 head

The entries therefore cut across barlines. The first replies overlap by
half a beat; later replies change the spacing. Their continuations diverge.
The exact three-beat template is stored for verification, but the five
occurrences are labeled as DERIVED MOTIVIC FRAGMENTS, not five additional
complete chant statements.

WHAT THE OTHER PARTS DO

Double bass retains its complete measured chant at 80–88. Its continuation
has an unequal harmonic rhythm rather than the previous quarter-note walk.
It moves through A-flat minor, C-flat, a passing first inversion and E-flat
minor, then opens toward G-flat. The tonic G-flat at 100 is approached
plagally. Only the later span 104–112 establishes the stronger D-flat-seventh
cadence into the quiet relative-major section.

Flute has a newly shaped falling ribbon above the short replies. Its large
upward leap waits until 96, after the initial relay, instead of placing the
highest register at the paragraph's first instant. At 110 it begins a held
F6, which continues across the bass's arrival at 112 and resolves to G-flat6
at 113. A provisional A-flat6 turn was removed during review to preserve
more register for the full piece's later B-flat6 crest.

Oboe completes its unchanged dance statement 76–84 before a breath and a
leaping answer. Its relative-major chant moves from 96 to 100. This aligns
the remembered melody with a local G-flat arrival and separates it from
the first upper leap.

Clarinet begins the retort, then takes a longer upward question and a fall.
At 100, a contracted rising companion replaces its old quarter-note ladder.

Violin postpones its retort until 82.5, then abandons the imitation for a
longer fall and a different turning phrase. At 111 it begins C-flat5 over
the dominant. That note crosses the new tonic at 112 and resolves down to
B-flat4 at 112.5.

Viola's opening continuation rises while cello falls; they no longer play
the same octave scale at 80–84. Its retort at 88 quickly gives way to a
different upward turn and a delayed six-five motion over the passing
B-flat bass. A broken-fourth answer later distinguishes it from the horn.

Horn alternates broad rising and falling gestures, then takes the retort
at 91 in a higher register. Its last A-flat3 is prepared over D-flat and
held through the arrival of G-flat; it resolves down to G-flat3 at 112.5.

Bassoon replaces its old scalar fall with concise turns and a held rebound.
It keeps the complete chant at 92–100. That measured statement supplies a
different timescale while the short retorts disperse.

Cello first opposes the viola, then takes the retort at 85 in its tenor
register. Its dance head at 88 is one octave above the earlier rendition's
E-flat2–B-flat2 position. The following phrase rises through G-flat3,
B-flat3, D-flat4 and E-flat4 while the oboe descends; an accidental octave
copy in the provisional draft was removed during independent review.

THE CADENCE OPENS IN STAGES

The bass reaches G-flat at 112 (1:02.222), while oboe, clarinet, bassoon and
cello also begin their new quieter phrases. Three continuing lines have
different resolutions:

- Violin C-flat5 resolves to B-flat4 at 112.5 (1:02.500).
- Horn A-flat3 resolves to G-flat3 at 112.5 (1:02.500).
- Flute F6 resolves upward to G-flat6 at 113 (1:02.778).

The horn leaves at 113, violin at 113.5, and flute at 114. The texture therefore
reduces from eight written lines immediately after the arrival to five in
three steps. This replaces the former single cutoff of the upper phrases.
The three sustained tones are actually carried across the boundary in the
score; they are not merely reattacked appoggiaturas described as ties.

INDEPENDENT REVIEW AND REPAIRS

The critic examined the literal notes and recommended targeted repairs:
- Raise the cello retort 85–88 an octave to avoid low F/G-flat collisions.
- Replace horn F3 at 94 with D-flat3 and clarinet D4 at 94.5 with D-flat4,
  clarifying the passing G-flat/B-flat sonority.
- Let bassoon rebound to G-flat3 at 86 rather than hold C-flat3 against a
  following low B-flat in horn.
- Use D-flat4 rather than C-flat4 in viola at 98, removing an unnecessarily
  persistent close C-flat/B-flat clash with horn while retaining its arc.
- Replace cello's provisional octave copy of oboe 92–95.5 with its own rise.
- Lower the provisional high flute turn at 109–110 to E-flat6–D-flat6.

The critic confirmed these changes and requested no further local repair.
The independent report should accompany this composition note.

LIMITS OF THE CLAIM

This is still nine-part writing with tonal extensions, chromatic/diatonic
friction, temporary unisons and octave convergences. It is not a certificate
of strict historical counterpoint. Short convergence at a cadence is
intentional; perpetual separation is not itself a musical virtue.

The new imitation gives a listener more specific material to follow, and
the register changes are intended to expose the replies. Whether these
changes actually achieve greater vitality, clarity, or emotional effect
must be judged in the recording. Individual pitches and rhythms cannot
establish that nine voices are perceived as nine musical personalities.

SCOPE AND REPRODUCIBILITY

The work remains 100 bars / 400 quarter beats in 4/4 at quarter = 108. All nine written
parts remain monophonic. The complete-form score contains 50 exact complete
thematic entries and 28 verified fragments, including the five new retorts.
The source checks verify notation and annotation consistency only.

The rewrite changes 80–112 in eight parts and 84–112 in oboe, preserving the
complete oboe statement 76–84. Flute, violin and horn extend to 114, 113.5 and
113 respectively. All events outside these declared windows are identical
to the preceding frozen score. The surrounding form and the later music
are preserved for a direct comparison.
