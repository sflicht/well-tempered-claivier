PRELUDE IV IN C-SHARP MINOR — RECOMPOSITION STUDY

Status: a newly composed audition draft. The prior canonical Prelude IV is
preserved. This report describes score-based decisions; it does not claim
that the rendered audio has been heard or that the piece reaches Bach's level.

The old problem
---------------
The previous prelude's opening used a broad cantabile melody above repeated
inner/bass chord figures. Its rhythmic energy changed by adding a different
texture: an eighth-note arpeggio at bar 9, then a semiquaver block at bar 17.
Those were changes of surface. They did not grow from an opening gesture
whose metre, continuation and harmonic pressure already demanded motion.

The new proposition
-------------------
32 bars of 3/4, quarter note = 96; 60 seconds of written music. Two complete
keyboard lines are literally authored, bar by bar. The code converts these
lines to timed events and notation. It does not choose pitches, solve chord
templates, or automatically repair them.

Opening upper line:
E5 eighth; C#5 and D#5 sixteenths; E5 eighth; G#5 dotted eighth;
F#5, E5, D#5 sixteenths.

Its duration pattern is 2,1,1,2,3,1,1,1 in sixteenth-note units. The upward
reach C#–D#–E–G# arrives at G# halfway through beat 2, holds it over the start
of beat 3, and releases through a descending tail. This displacement gives
the long note a destination and the short notes a continuation. It is not
an added accent pattern imposed on otherwise neutral notes.

The first answer continues D#–C#–B–A–G#, then turns B#–D#–B# into the next
C#. Against this descent the bass rises C#–E–F#–G#–A. The upper phrase and
bass line therefore determine one another's joints. The opening does not
restart its melody at the barline.

Formal and harmonic argument
----------------------------
Bars 1–4: establish C# minor. The upper descent and ascending bass reach A,
then the fourth bar turns F# minor toward the G# dominant. Its held F# over
the changed G# bass resolves down to E before the final dominant turn.

Bars 5–8: the bass takes the opening gesture. The upper line holds and
continues over that active line. The route to E major is specific: E over
B resolves to D# in bar 7; bar 8 prepares another E–D# 4–3 over B. The
last E in bar 8 anticipates the tonic so the upper line completes its
cadential turn before moving to G# at the new phrase. The
arrival at bar 9 has E in the bass and G# in the upper line: the third
remains on top, so this is an E-major arrival rather than a perfect cadence.

Bars 9–12: the gesture is recast in E major. The last E of bar 9 is tied
into bar 10, where it forms a seventh above F# and resolves to D#. B then
points back toward E; the C#-minor inflection in bar 11 turns to E7 through
the written D natural. Bar 12 answers on A and ends C#–D#–E#–F#, with E#
then F# against C# in the bass. This explicitly introduces the raised
leading note of F# minor and its resolution before the following phrase.

Bars 13–16: the idea takes root in F# minor. A second tied seventh, F# over
G#, resolves to E. The progression passes through the C# dominant, D major,
and B minor before returning to the C# dominant. These are phrase functions
supported by the written accidentals and bass, not labels applied to a
diatonic loop.

Bars 17–20: the bass answers again in the lower register. Bar 19 turns
toward A. The E tied into bar 20 resolves to D# above F#, recovering the
pitch collection of C# minor. The upper line then climbs E–F#–G#–A–B–C#–D#;
the bass ends G#–A–B. Both arrive at the original upper E and bass C# in
bar 21. This scalar expansion is the bridge into the return.

Bars 21–24: the first phrase returns recognisably. Its dominant ending
opens the final paragraph rather than supplying the final tonic.

Bars 25–29: C# rises to C#6, the highest note, over A: the expected tonic
closure is redirected toward VI. The bass then becomes more mobile, with
F#–D#–B–A–G#–F# in bar 26. Bar 27's held A forms a 4–3 over E, and its E
continues as a fourth above B before the following D#. Bar 29 gives the
actual D#–F#–A–C# components of the half-diminished predominant across the
two lines before moving into the dominant pedal.

Bars 30–32: the G# pedal supports the last ascent and descending reply.
The accented C# at bar 31 is a deliberate cadential 4–3 appoggiatura,
resolving to B#; it is not a prepared suspension. The final C# bass enters
one octave lower, and the upper E–D#–C# figure closes the phrase.

What makes the contrast audible in the score
-------------------------------------------
The opening and its F#-minor recasting retain the same recognisable rhythm.
The bass takes that rhythm in bars 5–6 and 17–18 while the upper line
continues in longer values. The three written ties into bars 10, 14 and 20
make harmonic tension cross a barline. Bar 20 expands the stepwise material
upward; bars 25–29 raise the register and increase the bass's rate of motion.
These changes give the return and final pedal different roles from the
opening. There is no inserted arpeggio section or unrelated fast-note block.

Technical review and its limits
-------------------------------
The descriptive event audit, before the final E4 anticipation in bar 8,
found no malformed/overlapping monophonic
events, no literal shared-attack parallel perfect intervals, no parallel
perfects on its quarter-note grid, and no outer-voice similar motion into
a perfect interval with an upper leap. Four strong-beat dissonances remain:
the three prepared sevenths at bars 10, 14 and 20, and the cadential 4–3
appoggiatura at bar 31. These have been reviewed at their actual notes.

Those checks are cleanup, not evidence of compelling music. This is a
compact, deliberately repetitive prelude study, with a literal four-bar
return and two principal lines. It does not solve the cycle's higher-part
fugal writing. Its musical success still needs to be judged by listening:
whether the cell has enough character, the sequences earn their length,
and the final paragraph feels like an intensified consequence.
