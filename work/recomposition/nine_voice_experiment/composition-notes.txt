THE PROCESSION FINDS A DANCE FLOOR
Nine-line chamber audition paragraph

This is a new experiment, not a completed fugue or a claim that the
twenty-five-part problem has been solved. The chamber version is the primary
audition. The piano version plays the same nine lines with one timbre; it is
a piano realization, not an arrangement for two human hands.

MATERIAL

1. The remembered procession

The opening eight pitches of the traditional Dies irae are F-E-F-D-E-C-D-D.
Here their measured durations, in quarter beats, are
1.5, 0.5, 1, 1, 0.5, 0.5, 1, 2. That measured rhythm is new; the pitch
fragment is borrowed. The horn first presents it at beat 0.

2. The interruption

The original dance head is D-E-F / A-G-F / D-A. Its first bar groups the
eighth-note pulse as 3+3+2; the A-G recoil is dotted and short. Its second
bar starts with a rest, then C-B-flat-A / F-E-D-C. The violin enters with
this idea at beat 2, halfway through the opening bar.

3. The bridge

The original broad line is A-D-E-F-A / G-E-C. It first appears in the viola
against the horn's chant. Its fourth, stepwise rise, high point, and fall
become material for longer and shorter continuations; it is not a permanent
accompaniment pattern.

THE MUSICAL SITUATION

0:00-0:09, beats 0-16: The procession is interrupted.
Horn chant and viola bridge begin together. The violin interrupts at beat 2;
the clarinet, cello, oboe, and bassoon enter at different phrase positions.
The flute arrives with only the dance head at beat 12.

0:09-0:18, beats 16-32: Nine players; their functions change.
The flute adopts the chant while the oboe takes the dance. The viola follows
the flute's chant one beat later. The horn's dance begins at beat 18.
Clarinet has the original bridge; violin has a differently timed ascent and
fall; cello's twelve-beat arch crosses the next bar group before a late
dance head; the double bass bends its own bridge down and back up toward
its high-register chant.

0:18-0:27, beats 32-48: The wrong turn.
The viola's E-natural at 32 becomes E-flat at 33 and D at 34. That chromatic
descent travels through horn at 33.5/34/35 and bassoon at 34/35/36. The
flattened second spreads into the chant and dance. The double bass descends
through the register, then reverses direction and climbs.

0:27-0:36, beats 48-64: The procession hurries.
Flute gives the chant head in half durations at 48; violin follows at 49.
Horn starts the dance head at 48, double bass at 49, cello at 50. The cello
has a distinct C-D-E-C approach so it does not prematurely double the horn
head. Each of those low lines has a different continuation. F-sharp and
C-sharp begin entering during the second half of this paragraph.

0:36-0:40, beats 64-72: The procession changes its mind.
The oboe's chant is now major, while the flute plays the major dance. Viola
has the hurried major chant. Bass begins the major bridge; clarinet follows
a beat later in a different tonal position and carries its phrase beyond
beat 72. Violin descends, horn climbs by steps, and cello climbs from its
held D to its largest high point. Flute and bassoon briefly share the dance
head in octaves at 64-68; this is a shared arrival gesture, not independent
counterpoint between those two lines during those four beats.

0:40-0:44.4, beats 72-80: Release.
Different ascending and descending continuations arrive at a brief final
D-major sonority. The clarinet completes its late bridge and joins through
a seven-beat continuation. The final two beats are deliberately chordal.

ORCHESTRATION AND ARTICULATION

Flute, oboe, clarinet, violin, viola, horn, bassoon, cello, and double bass
have explicit individual timbres and stable stereo positions. The horn and
cello are separated for their delayed pursuit, with the bass near center.
The high winds and horn start softer than the strings and clarinet/bassoon.

The chant is deadpan and lightly tenuto; the dance is clipped with weight
at its 3+3+2 group beginnings; the bridge has a longer arc toward its high
point. For a head following a pickup, the accent origin follows the actual
head, not the start of the larger phrase. The ninth entrance is comparatively
light; density alone does not trigger a large dynamic accent.

WHAT REVIEW CHANGED

The first literal checkpoint had a real defect: at beat 16, flute and viola
doubled the chant in octaves while four other lines gave synchronized
transpositions of the bridge. A similar parallel-family alignment occurred
at the major return. That made too much of the intended many-line texture
into three harmonized patterns.

Those particular phrases were rewritten. The delayed viola, long cello
continuation, bent bass arch, and independently shaped violin line now
separate the first full texture. At the return, a late clarinet entry and
different horn, cello, and violin directions replace the simultaneous
parallel arches. The delayed low entries and the traveling chromatic
descent were retained. The cello approach, actual accent origins, and
horn/cello stereo separation were also revised.

LIMITS AND THE AUDITION QUESTION

The piece permits modal dissonance, register crossings, occasional parallel
gestures, and a brief chordal arrival. It was not flattened to satisfy the
earlier triad-only counterpoint screen. All nine parts were authored as
phrases and remained revisable; no fixed three-part core was filled with
additional chord tones.

That construction method is not evidence that the combination sounds good.
Some family rhythms still align deliberately, and high density may obscure
the most useful relationships. The two-bar phrase families may still be too
prominent; the major-mode turn may sound like a cheap joke instead of an
earned release. Those are material audition risks, not resolved questions.

The most informative passage to hear is beats 32-64: does the traveling
E-E-flat-D descent create pressure, and do the overlapping hurried chant and
low dance entries make the music more alive? If their interaction is merely
busy, this paragraph needs recomposition. Nine tracks and thematic labels
would not establish otherwise.
