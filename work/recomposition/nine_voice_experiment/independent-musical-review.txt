Independent review of the nine-line audition experiment

DECISION

The revised checkpoint is suitable for audition. It contains nine authored
lines which exchange and overlap three ideas, including genuine imitative
passages and differing phrase trajectories. It is not a completed fugue or
proof of continuous nine-part independence. Its harmonic density and the
audibility of the competing gestures remain questions for listening.

The title now describes a concrete musical situation. A newly measured chant,
a dry dance with a 3+3+2 accent pattern, and a broad rising idea can interrupt
and transform one another. This gives the writing a more specific character
than the previous cycle's interchangeable exposition/episode/tutti machinery.
The material still sometimes moves in phrase families, but the revisions
below prevent the first full ensemble from simply presenting three harmonized
layers under nine instrumental names.

WHAT THE INITIAL CHECKPOINT GOT WRONG, AND WHAT CHANGED

At q16 the first version doubled the entire flute chant in the viola two
octaves below it. Clarinet, violin, cello and double bass simultaneously
played transposed versions of the same rising phrase, with the same rhythm
and contour. Hand-authoring those notes did not, by itself, make their
combined texture independent. A similar group of aligned rising phrases
returned at q64. These were substantive echoes of the old method's problem.

The revised q16 texture is materially different. The viola continues its
descent, enters the chant at q17 and carries it across q24. The cello takes
twelve beats to complete a rise and descent before its next dance head at
q28. The violin has its own differently timed ascent and turn; the bass
bends its opening arch downward before rising toward its next entry.
The clarinet alone retains the original broad rising shape at q16. Thus
the accompanying voices now have different destinations and different
times of reaching them, rather than merely different starting pitches.

At q64 the bass alone starts the original major-mode bridge. The clarinet
begins its bridge at q65, crosses q72 and continues at q73. The violin
descends against the returning flute dance; horn and cello take distinct
routes upward. In particular, the cello leaves its preceding held D3,
rises through F#3–A3–B3–C#4 to F#4 and then descends. This gives its earlier
low material a real registral development.

THE STRONGEST ACTUAL INTERACTIONS

The E-flat turn has a melodic cause. The viola passes E4–Eb4–D4 at q32–34;
the horn follows through E3–Eb3–D3, with Eb at q34; the bassoon reaches Eb3
at q35 before descending onward. The adjacent forms of E rub against one
another while the semitone descent moves through the ensemble. Those are
directed passing tensions rather than an arbitrary permission to use any
pitch. At q33, for example, the A bass and F/C/Eb/G upper content make a
specific dominant-ninth color over A. The harmony changes as the lines
continue, rather than staying inside one redistributed triad.

The q48–56 paragraph offers the clearest imitative interplay. The flute
hurries the chant at q48; the violin pursues it at q49. Concurrently, the
dance head enters horn at q48, double bass at q49 and cello at q50. These
participants then continue differently. This is a particular musical event,
not simply nine simultaneous versions of a rhythm template.

One local defect was corrected during review. The cello's original q48
pickup was D3–E3–F3–G3, duplicating the horn's first three notes at the same
time and prematurely announcing most of the purported third entry. The new
C3–D3–E3–C3 approach separates its preparation from the D3 head at q50.
The revised accent origins also follow the delayed heads, so the performed
3+3+2 stresses belong to each entrance rather than the enclosing phrase box.

REMAINING LIMITS AND LISTENING QUESTIONS

Some two-bar organization and simultaneous family entries remain, notably
the chant variants around q24 and the broad arches around q40. They can
provide local coherence, but the danger is hearing continuous modal texture
instead of particular voices changing one another's course. Different solo
melodies are not sufficient evidence of a successful combined composition.

The flute and bassoon still play the major dance head in exact octaves from
q64 to q68. They then diverge: the bassoon gives its next four beats to the
hurried chant. That one-bar agreement can function as an arrival gesture;
it should not be represented as nine independently moving parts at every
instant. Likewise, the final two-beat D-major chord is a shared resolution,
not an achievement of counterpoint merely because all nine players sound.

The ensemble frequently fills much of the modal collection. This is a
different harmonic language from the old all-triad texture, but saturation
could flatten the contrast between buildup, disruption and release. The
important listening question is whether the E-flat disturbance and later
F-sharp/C-sharp turn actually change the perceived direction, and whether
the first clear common arrival feels earned. Additional activity or more
semitone clashes would not automatically improve either result.

No further fatal literal-note issue warrants delaying the first audition.
The defensible present claim is a playful nine-line contrapuntal experiment
with several specific interactions, revised to remove an exposed instance
of grouped harmony masquerading as independence. The actual expressive
success of those interactions remains open.
