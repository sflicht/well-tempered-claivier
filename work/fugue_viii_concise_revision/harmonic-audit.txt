Scope: written score inspection; no audio listening and no historical-style certification.
Quarter-note beats are zero-based; bar = floor(beat/4) + 1.

MAIN FINDING

The user's distinction between improved counterpoint and unsatisfactory harmony
is supported by the notes. The later rewrite gives several parts recognizable
motivic work, but many untouched continuations simply traverse the same scale
at similar speeds. Combining them often yields most of a diatonic collection
rather than a clearly directed chord with a few intelligible moving tensions.
Some collisions are also literal major/minor versions of the same degree.
The chamber samples can expose these problems more harshly, but timbre alone
cannot account for them. The piano-first decision is sensible; the score still
needs harmonic decisions.

PRIORITY REPAIRS IN THE RETAINED BEATS 0–112

1. Beat 39, bar 10: simultaneous C4 and Cb4.
   Viola attacks C4 for one beat; bassoon attacks Cb4 for half a beat.
   Both are new attacks, and the viola's raised sixth has no compelling local
   function. The surrounding pitches are Eb5, Bb5, F4, F3 and Db3.
   Simple candidate repair: viola C4 -> Cb4. This makes its preceding Db4
   descend by a whole step and avoids an unexplained split sixth. Brief
   unison with bassoon is preferable to preserving an accidental conflict.

2. Beats 43.5–46, bar 11 into bar 12: an unclear first dominant cadence.
   At 43.5 clarinet C4 overlaps bassoon Cb3. At 45 oboe D5 and then viola D4
   at 45.5 overlap bassoon Db3 for a full beat. Thus the first explicit major
   dominant third is opposed by its minor version. This is not merely the
   piano's decay: these pitches coexist in the written durations.
   Candidate repairs:
   - Clarinet beat 43 C4 -> Cb4, producing Db4–Cb4–Bb3 at 42–44.
   - Bassoon beat 45 Db3 -> D3, producing Bb2–D3–F3–Bb2 at 44–48.
   - Recompose the surrounding 40–48 bass/horn route to establish Bb7
     clearly. Horn Gb3 at 45 and cello F2 beneath the bass Ab2 blur the
     intended hierarchy. Do not merely change all pitches to a chord;
     decide which moving notes prepare or resolve against the Bb dominant.
   Beat 40 also sustains cello Cb3 for two beats above Bb1 in the bass.
   A flat ninth could be intentional, but this one arrives before the
   dominant's identity is clear, while several unrelated passing tones pile up.

3. Beats 60–72, bars 16–18: repeated low-middle clustering.
   Horn F3 at 60–62 collides with bassoon Gb3 at 61–62.
   At 67, horn and viola both have Gb3, bassoon F3 and cello Ab3.
   At 68, bassoon Eb3, viola F3, cello Gb3 and horn Ab3 sound together
   for half a beat, with three of those notes continuing for a full beat.
   The bass holds Eb2 at 68–70. This is the entire first four scale degrees
   compressed into the low-middle register, rather than one clear suspension
   against a stable harmonic implication.
   Repair the inner paragraph as a combination, not as isolated seconds.
   Keep the violin chant and a purposeful bass; give horn a slower route,
   move the cello continuation into a distinct tenor arch, and let one of
   the remaining middle parts breathe or answer later. Raising one line
   can reduce roughness, but does not alone give the aggregate a function.
   The repeated quarter-note scalar continuations here are also a strong
   source of the user's harmonic and rhythmic sameness objection.

4. Beat 78, bar 20: D-natural bass against Db in several other lines.
   Bass D2 lasts one beat. Horn Db3 also lasts one beat; the oboe passes
   through Db5 at 78.5. Cello holds Cb3, and several other lines hold Eb.
   This sounds on paper like two different cadence plans superimposed:
   Bb-major dominant leading tone versus the natural-minor thematic scale.
   Choose a route. If D2 stays, adapt the conflicting notes to a Bb-dominant
   environment, including a tonal alteration of the oboe's dance reply if
   necessary. If the Db material is important, change the bass and use an
   explicit Db7 approach to the upcoming Gb bass at 80. Do not retain the
   written clash solely to preserve a literal-transposition annotation.

5. Beats 80–112: retain the new retort, but clarify its harmonic support.
   The three-beat retort and staggered arrivals are the most specific work
   in the retained paragraph. A broad route can be heard in the written
   bass: Eb minor / Gb at 80, Ab minor around 88, Cb at 92, Ab minor at 96,
   Gb at 100, Ab-minor predominant at 104, Db7 at 108, Gb at 112.
   Several of the new passing dissonances are short and directionally
   meaningful. Do not erase them with a blanket consonance rule.
   Priority is the supporting free continuations. At 84 the bass F2 is
   joined by F3/F4/F5 plus Eb and Ab, so it lacks a clear third/fifth
   combination. At 85 Eb bass is loaded with F, Gb, Ab and Cb as well as
   Eb. These are possible extended sonorities, but their density makes the
   phrase sound more modal and static than cadential. Assign a specific
   passing inversion or predominant to 84–86 and adjust the longer notes
   accordingly, while retaining the short retort's profile.
   The 108–114 cadence is comparatively coherent: Gb4 approaches F4 over
   Db, Cb participates in the dominant seventh, and the delayed upper
   resolutions make the arrival unfold. Keep its useful directional work.

FORM AND TONAL DIRECTION

A 64-bar condensation is a stronger response than trimming a few seconds.
Keep the exposition and improved retort; shorten the long relative-major
excursion to a genuine respite with a cadence. Then give the music a clear
Ab-minor predominant -> Bb dominant -> Eb-minor return. A compressed chase
can intensify that return, followed by a concise cadence, perhaps with a
brief Picardy change. The original sixteen-bar major concluding region
creates a separate final act after several previous climaxes and returns.

The existing 176–240 chromatic paragraph is an especially good deletion or
recomposition candidate. Its Fb-colored material often meets unchanged Eb
lines directly: at 188 cello Eb3 lasts two beats against bassoon Fb3 and
bass Fb2; at 200 clarinet Eb3 attacks against horn Fb3. At 232 both violin
and horn Cb4 collide with viola/bassoon Bb3 above the Bb dominant. These
are prolonged enough to be compositional decisions, not MIDI defects.
The planned condensation can remove the detour without losing the best
new retort passage.

WTC DIRECTION AS A COMPOSITIONAL BRIEF

The present work's long near-modal regions, two contrasting thematic worlds,
low-register scalar crowds, repeated late returns and prolonged major
apotheosis collectively place it well away from the stated WTC starting
point. Bringing it nearer should mean more consequential tonal answers,
sequential episodes that actually move to a new region, dissonances whose
preparation and destination can be followed, and a tighter return/cadence.
A harpsichord patch, trill ornaments, or uniform terraced dynamics would not
by themselves make those decisions.

These are score-grounded candidates. They are not proof of what the user
heard at a particular instant, and the resulting revision needs audition.
