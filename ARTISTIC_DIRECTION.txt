THE WELL-TEMPERED clAIvier
An artistic direction for the AI Fugue

I want this cycle to contain music with appetite: pieces that dance, argue,
brood, misbehave, suddenly become tender, and sometimes reach for grandeur.
The twenty-four keys and increasing number of voices give the collection
its frame. They should leave room for forty-eight different experiences.

The proposed AI Fugue is music in which recognizable ideas acquire lives
in several voices, and those voices change one another's course. It keeps
the pleasure of following an idea through transformations and encounters.
It can extend those encounters beyond the speed, reach, coordination and
memory a human performer or ensemble could manage. Its ambitions still
have to reach a listener.

THE MUSIC IS ALLOWED TO HAVE A SENSE OF HUMOUR

A grand phrase can be interrupted by an impertinent little figure. A
familiar cadence can turn out to be the entrance of a different subject.
A melody may briefly resemble an old friend and then go somewhere absurd.
An awkward AI-generated scrap may contain a rhythm worth rescuing. A
beautiful tune can also be allowed to remain beautiful without proving
its cleverness at every bar.

Original melodies, folk tunes, chant, dance habits, quotations and allusions
are available material. They are options to choose from piece by piece.
There is no quota of novelty or borrowing. Any borrowed material should
be credited accurately, and its transformation should contribute to the
piece's character. A title explaining a joke cannot replace the audible joke.

THE VOICES NEED MUSICAL LIVES

One voice can carry a long breath while another dances around it. A third
can interrupt, inherit a fragment, or postpone a resolution. Later, their
roles can reverse. The counterpoint grows when a new line changes the
possibilities of the others. It is perfectly acceptable for the whole
ensemble to hit a chord, fall silent, or suddenly agree; the surrounding
lines must give that event its meaning.

The increasing part count remains a real compositional ambition. A
twenty-five-part work needs substantial twenty-five-part counterpoint.
There is no requirement that every part be equally prominent or constantly
busy. Repetition, imitation, doubling and pedals can all serve the music,
but duplicating a musical function does not by itself fulfill the promise
of another independent line.

TIME AND SPACE CAN BECOME COMPOSITIONAL MATERIAL

An idea might unfold slowly in one line while a quicker version crosses
it; several rhythmic cycles might drift apart and meet at a decisive
moment. The register can exceed a pianist's hands. A phrase can move
between distant players. None of these devices is compulsory, and none
should be used simply to advertise difficulty. Their combinations must
be composed and heard, with intentional voice leading and dissonance.

Spatial placement and orchestration can help attention travel through
the ensemble. The eventual monumental interior can make the experience
immersive. The musical event still has to exist in the notes; a camera
move or a cathedral reverberation cannot invent it afterwards.

FORM SHOULD FOLLOW THE PARTICULAR ARGUMENT

Sam's Beethoven reference points toward fugal writing used creatively
within a larger musical course. A dance, a lament, a confrontation and
a gradual convergence should not all receive the same exposition,
episode and climax. Some pieces may be compact and lucid. Others may
change direction several times before their materials discover how to
coexist. The final convergence can acquire force because the listener
has learned the separate ideas and can recognize what has changed.

The desired ecstasy should have many approaches. A quiet expansion of
space may prepare it; so may a ridiculous little rhythm that gradually
takes over, or a phrase that keeps reaching beyond its expected ending.
Constant solemnity would deprive the cycle of much of its expressive range.

CURRENT MUSICAL WORK

The promising existing preludes, particularly III and VIII, are retained
for selective development. The shared fugue-generation method is retired.
Two fresh, deliberately modest studies establish a different approach to
gesture and phrase: the two-part Fugue I and the new Prelude IV.

A separate nine-player experiment now tests a much more playful encounter:
a measured chant-like procession meets an original, sharply accented dance
figure. This is one chosen experiment, not a template for the other works.
Its success has to be judged from the actual music. It is not the finished
Fugue VIII and does not establish the twenty-five-part finale.

These notes are a direction, not an examination syllabus. The practical
task is to compose, discard weak material, retain what has life, and let
the individual pieces become recognizably themselves.
